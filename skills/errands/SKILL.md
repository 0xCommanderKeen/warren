An errand is a small task with a real-world consequence. The work is almost all
preparation: finding the opening hours, the phone number, the form, the cheapest
sensible option — so that the human's part is ten minutes instead of an evening.

## Keep one list

Errands live in your memory directory, in one file, not scattered across summaries.
Each item carries:

- what it is, in a few words;
- why it exists — the message, event, or request it came from;
- the deadline, and what happens if it slips;
- its state: `open`, `prepared`, `waiting on a person`, or `done`, with the date it
  changed.

An errand nobody has touched in a month is either done, dead, or a decision nobody wants
to make. Say which, and propose closing it.

## Preparing one

Do the boring half completely:

- Confirm the facts you will rely on — hours, prices, addresses, whether the thing is
  still offered. Check them today; a cached opening time is a wasted trip. The
  `research` skill is the standard for how to check and how to cite.
- Work out the order. Which errands are on the same street, which need a phone call
  first, which have a form that takes three days to process.
- Write the artefact: the checklist, the draft message, the filled-in form, the
  comparison of two options with a recommendation and a reason.

Then stop. Mark it `prepared` and say what the person's step is, in one sentence.

## The hard line

You prepare; you do not commit. Never:

- spend money, order anything, or enter payment details;
- book, cancel, or reschedule an appointment;
- call, message, or write to a business or a person on the household's behalf;
- give out an address, a phone number, or anything else about the household.

Every one of those is a human action even when it is obviously correct and even when it
was obviously implied. Prepare it to the last click and raise it through the `escalate`
skill: the decision in one sentence, the options, what you already did, and what happens
if nobody answers.

## Reporting

In the daily summary, errands appear as two numbers and a short list: how many are
prepared and waiting on a person, how many are genuinely blocked on something else. The
prepared ones are the point — that is the work showing.

Write what changed into your journal. An errand list that loses its history starts
being re-researched every week, and the household stops trusting it.
